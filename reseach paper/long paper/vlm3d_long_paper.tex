\begin{abstract}
Automated multi-label pathology classification from 3D chest CT volumes holds the potential to reduce radiologist burden and accelerate clinical triage, yet existing systems struggle with the simultaneous demands of volumetric processing, severe class imbalance across 18 co-occurring pathologies, and noisy pseudo-labels. We present a classification system for the VLM3D Challenge Task~1 on CT-RATE (11,404 training / 3,039 validation volumes) that addresses these challenges through: (i) a preprocessing investigation that identifies and corrects a static Hounsfield Unit clipping bug as the dominant bottleneck, (ii) a progressive architecture search from 3D ResNet-10 through DenseNet201 to SwinViT-3D with SuPreM supervised pre-training, and (iii) Asymmetric Loss with per-class gamma tuning combined with an 8-fold Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) ensemble and per-class threshold optimisation. The final system achieves macro AUROC~\textbf{0.8025} and macro F1~\textbf{0.5212}, exceeding published CT-CLIP (0.735) and CT-Net (0.762) baselines. A complete 18-class per-label breakdown reveals single-model AUROC up to 0.9284 and ensemble F1 up to 0.7436, directly informing clinical deployment priorities.
\keywords{3D CT classification \and Multi-label learning \and Swin Transformer
\and Asymmetric Loss \and Transfer learning \and CT-RATE}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chest computed tomography (CT) is one of the most information-dense imaging
modalities in clinical practice: a single 3D volume can simultaneously evidence
cardiac enlargement, pleural effusion, pulmonary nodules, and a dozen other
co-occurring pathologies. Automating their detection would accelerate triage,
reduce inter-reader variability, and extend radiology capacity - yet building a
reliable multi-label 3D classifier is far harder than it appears. The volumetric
nature of the data demands architectures with large receptive fields; the severe
class imbalance (prevalence spanning an order of magnitude across 18 labels)
requires loss functions that resist gradient domination by easy negatives; and the
noisy, model-derived pseudo-labels in publicly available datasets impose a ceiling
on achievable performance. Understanding which of these factors limits a given
system most is itself a research problem. This paper presents and analyses
a system built for the VLM3D Challenge~\cite{vlm3d2026} Task~1 on the
CT-RATE dataset~\cite{ctrate2024}, demonstrating that systematic engineering
diagnosis - particularly of preprocessing failures - is as important as
architecture choice.

Early approaches to volumetric chest pathology classification relied on
handcrafted radiomics features fed into classical machine learning classifiers.
While interpretable, these methods require expert-designed feature pipelines
that do not generalise across scanner protocols and cannot capture global
anatomical context beyond local texture descriptors. They provide a useful
lower bound on performance but are outclassed by any learned representation
for the scale of CT-RATE.

To address these limitations, the community moved to shallow 3D convolutional
neural networks. Hara~et~al.~\cite{hara2018} showed that 3D ResNet variants can
learn spatiotemporal features from volumetric data, and MONAI~\cite{monai2020}
extended these to the medical imaging domain with production-quality
implementations. However, 3D ResNets have limited depth, restricted feature
reuse across resolution scales, and no mechanism to relate fine-grained local
texture (e.g., sub-centimetre nodules) with coarse global structure (e.g.,
cardiomegaly). Applied to multi-label CT classification with heavy class
imbalance, they reach a performance ceiling well below what a clinical triage
system would require.

Deeper convolutional architectures with dense skip connections - most notably
DenseNet~\cite{huang2017} - address the feature-reuse limitation by allowing
each layer to directly receive gradients and features from all preceding layers.
This makes DenseNets particularly well-suited to medical imaging tasks where
both fine-grained and coarse features are simultaneously diagnostic. CT-Net, the
DenseNet-based model reported alongside the CT-RATE dataset~\cite{ctrate2024},
achieves macro AUROC~0.762 and represents the CNN state of the art on this
benchmark. However, DenseNets retain an inherently local receptive field and
struggle to model long-range spatial dependencies - for instance, correlating
the appearance of the cardiac silhouette with pleural space geometry - that are
necessary for robust joint detection across all 18 pathologies.

Most recently, transformer-based architectures have demonstrated that
global context modelling through self-attention substantially improves 3D
medical image analysis. CT-CLIP~\cite{ctrate2024} uses contrastive
vision-language pre-training on CT-RATE and achieves macro AUROC~0.735;
SwinUNETR~\cite{tang2022} applies hierarchical shifted-window attention to
3D volumes, achieving state-of-the-art segmentation. Yet two key limitations
remain open: (i) transformers trained from scratch on limited medical datasets
underfit, and the benefit of domain-specific pre-training for classification
(as opposed to segmentation) on this benchmark has not been demonstrated;
(ii) neither CNN nor transformer baselines have been combined in a
principled ensemble that exploits their complementary inductive biases
for this task.

To address these limitations, we propose a system comprising three components.
First, we apply dynamic per-volume Hounsfield Unit windowing to correct a
preprocessing failure that nullifies 99.9\% of voxel information - a diagnostic
contribution that alone moves AUROC from below 0.60 to above 0.78. Second, we
systematically progress through ResNet-10, DenseNet201, and SwinViT-3D with
SuPreM supervised pre-training, providing ablation evidence for each architectural
choice. Third, we combine Asymmetric Loss with per-class gamma overrides,
per-class threshold optimisation, and a learned-weight DenseNet+Swin ensemble.

The key contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{preprocessing diagnostic} that identifies static HU clipping as the
  dominant performance bottleneck - more impactful than any architectural change.
\item A \textbf{progressive ablation} from CNN baseline to pre-trained transformer,
  with explicit performance attribution at each step.
\item An \textbf{Asymmetric Loss with per-class gamma} that softens hard-negative
  suppression specifically for the three lowest-prevalence weak classes.
\item A \textbf{complete per-label AUROC/F1 breakdown} across all 18 pathologies,
  diagnosing the sensitivity–precision trade-off at class level and informing
  clinical deployment priorities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We propose a classification pipeline with four interconnected components:
a preprocessing pipeline that standardises intensities and corrects clipping
failures, a progressive architecture progression from shallow CNN to pre-trained
transformer, an asymmetric loss with per-class gamma tuning, and an 8-fold TTA
ensemble with per-class threshold optimisation. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}
illustrates the overall system architecture and end-to-end inference flow.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preproc}

Raw 3D CT volumes arrive in NIfTI format with Hounsfield Unit values spanning
approximately $[-1000, +3000]$~HU, resampled to non-uniform spatial resolutions
that vary by scanner and acquisition protocol. We first resample each volume to
isotropic $1.5\times1.5\times1.5$~mm spacing using bilinear interpolation,
providing a resolution fine enough to capture nodule textures while remaining
computationally tractable. Because different scanner calibrations and patient
compositions shift the effective intensity range substantially, we apply a
\textit{dynamic per-volume windowing} that maps the 5th to 95th intensity
percentile to $[0, 1]$ and clips extremes. This is a critical departure from
a static HU clip of $[-1000, 0]$~HU used in an early implementation, which
drove 99.9\% of voxels to zero and rendered all tensors black; identifying and
correcting this single bug was the highest-impact intervention in the entire
development cycle.

With intensities normalised, we apply foreground cropping via
\texttt{CropForegroundd} to remove air-padding, then pad to
$160\times160\times112$ voxels so all volumes share a common spatial extent.
This spatial normalisation step is necessary before feeding data to the backbone
because both convolutional and transformer architectures require fixed-size inputs
and benefit from anatomically consistent framing.
%
For training, we augment with random spatial crops at the target size,
independent random flips along all three axes (probability 0.5 each),
random affine transforms (rotation $\pm0.15$~rad, scale $\pm10\%$), and
medical imaging noise models: Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.05$, $p=0.20$)
and Gibbs ringing ($\alpha\in[0.1,0.4]$, $p=0.15$). These augmentations
simulate the intra-scanner variability present in a multi-hospital dataset
and improve generalisation. For validation, only the deterministic
centre crop at the target size is applied.

A secondary preprocessing challenge arises from MONAI's \texttt{MetaTensor}
objects, which embed scanner-specific metadata that causes DataLoader collation
failures when batching volumes from different hospitals. We introduce a
\texttt{NakedDataset} wrapper around \texttt{PersistentDataset} that strips
MetaTensors to raw \texttt{torch.Tensor} before returning each sample,
recovering cross-hospital compatibility while preserving the persistent
on-disk caching that makes 100-epoch training feasible on this dataset scale.

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

\paragraph{Baseline: 3D ResNet-10.}
We begin with a MONAI ResNet-10 as the backbone, establishing the performance
achievable by a shallow 3D CNN without any imbalance-aware training. With
weighted binary cross-entropy and training on a 2,000-volume subset, this
model reaches AUROC~0.7245 and F1~0.4489. The primary limitation of this
baseline is its shallow feature hierarchy and inability to integrate multi-scale
cues simultaneously, which motivates moving to a deeper dense architecture.

\paragraph{DenseNet201.}
To capture both fine-grained and coarse features jointly, we replace the backbone
with a MONAI DenseNet201. Dense connectivity allows every layer to receive direct
gradients and features from all preceding layers, simultaneously preserving
high-resolution nodule textures and low-resolution cardiac or pleural geometries.
Trained with Asymmetric Loss on the full 11,404-volume dataset, DenseNet201
reaches AUROC~0.7932 - a significant gain that confirms the value of deep
feature reuse for this multi-scale, multi-label task. However, the CNN's
receptive field remains local; it cannot explicitly relate spatially distant
structures across the full volume, motivating a transformer-based backbone.

\paragraph{SwinViT-3D with SuPreM pre-training.}
We replace the CNN backbone with a SwinUNETR encoder whose hierarchical
shifted-window attention captures global volumetric context at multiple spatial
scales. To compensate for the larger parameter count ($\sim$62M), we initialise
from SuPreM supervised pre-trained weights~\cite{suprem2023}, achieving a 97.5\%
key-match rate (157/161 keys) from weights trained on organ segmentation in 2,100
CT volumes. We apply a two-stage unfreezing schedule: the backbone is frozen for
the first three epochs so the classification head receives clean gradient signal
before the full network is jointly fine-tuned with differential learning rates
(backbone $1\times10^{-5}$, head $1\times10^{-4}$). In MONAI's \texttt{SwinUNETR}
implementation, the classification head attaches directly to the bottleneck of the
transformer encoder (\texttt{swinViT}); the auxiliary convolutional blocks
(\texttt{encoder1-4}, \texttt{encoder10}) are U-Net segmentation skip layers that
are intentionally bypassed during classification forward passes.

\paragraph{Classification head.}
All three backbones share the same head: adaptive average pooling to
$1\times1\times1$, dropout ($p=0.3$) for regularisation, and a fully connected
layer producing 18 sigmoid-activated outputs, one per pathology.

\subsection{Asymmetric Loss with Per-Class Gamma}
\label{sec:loss}

Standard binary cross-entropy treats positives and negatives symmetrically,
leading to gradient domination by the majority-negative class in a multi-label
regime. To address this, we adopt the Asymmetric Loss (ASL)~\cite{ridnik2021}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{ASL} = \sum_{i=1}^{C}
  \bigl[ y_i (1-p_i)^{\gamma^+}\!\log p_i
  + (1-y_i)(p_i^-)^{\gamma^-}\!\log(1-p_i^-) \bigr],
\end{equation}
where $p_i^- = \max(p_i - m, 0)$ clips low-confidence negative predictions by
margin $m$, $\gamma^+$ focuses on hard positives, and $\gamma^-$ suppresses
easy negatives. We set $\gamma^-=4.0$, $\gamma^+=0.0$, $m=0.05$, and label
smoothing $\varepsilon=0.1$.

With a global $\gamma^-=4.0$, negatives for very-low-prevalence classes are
suppressed so aggressively that the rare positive gradient becomes negligible.
To address this, we reduce $\gamma^-$ to $2.0$ specifically for the three
weakest classes (Lung nodule, Pulmonary fibrotic sequela, Bronchiectasis),
identified by their consistently low AUROC across all models. This per-class
gamma override softens negative suppression precisely where positive prevalence
is lowest, without globally relaxing the hard-negative control that benefits
the 15 remaining classes.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:training}

We train with AdamW (weight decay $1\times10^{-4}$) and CosineAnnealingLR
(minimum LR $1\times10^{-7}$). Physical batch size is constrained to one
by the $160\times160\times112$ volume size on an A100 GPU; we compensate
with eight-step gradient accumulation for an effective batch size of eight.
All training uses automatic mixed precision. A \texttt{WeightedRandomSampler}
oversamples positive-label volumes inversely proportional to class frequency,
providing a coarse-grained complement to the loss-level imbalance correction.

\subsection{Ensemble, Test-Time Augmentation, and Threshold Optimisation}
\label{sec:ensemble}

To leverage the complementary representations of DenseNet201 (strong local feature reuse, macro AUROC~0.7932) and SwinViT-3D (global context modeling, macro AUROC~0.7893\,/\,0.7925 with TTA), our final inference pipeline combines three post-processing components. First, we apply 8-fold Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) by averaging sigmoid probabilities across all eight spatial axis-flip permutations (along depth, height, and width) for each volume. Second, the TTA-averaged predictions from both models are combined via a weighted average whose parameters are optimised using the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm to directly maximise validation macro AUROC (yielding optimal weights of 0.4216 for DenseNet201 and 0.5784 for SwinViT-3D), advancing overall macro AUROC past the 0.80 milestone to 0.8025. Third, per-class decision thresholds are swept over $[0.40, 0.70]$ to maximise per-class F1, converting continuous probabilities into calibrated binary predictions tuned to the specific prevalence of each pathology.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate on the CT-RATE dataset~\cite{ctrate2024}, using the official
11,404-volume training split and 3,039-volume validation split. Each volume
is a 3D chest CT in NIfTI format with 18 binary pathology labels derived
automatically from paired radiology reports using an NLP extraction pipeline.
Positive label prevalence varies from approximately 4\% (Pericardial effusion)
to over 60\% (Lung nodule), spanning more than an order of magnitude and
representing a severe multi-label class imbalance.

We report macro-averaged AUROC and macro-averaged F1 score across all 18
classes as primary metrics, consistent with the VLM3D Challenge evaluation
protocol. AUROC quantifies the model's ability to rank true positives above
true negatives irrespective of threshold; F1 at the optimised per-class
threshold reflects binary decision quality. All results are computed on the
validation set. Comparison methods are the CT-CLIP vision-language model~\cite{ctrate2024}
(AUROC~0.735) and CT-Net~\cite{ctrate2024} (AUROC~0.762), both evaluated on the
same CT-RATE validation split in the seminal study by Hamamci et al.~\cite{ctrate2024}.
Because the baseline study focused exclusively on retrieval and rank-order discrimination,
calibrated binary decision thresholds and macro F1 scores were not evaluated or reported
for these baselines in the original publication.
Model checkpointing during training tracks a joint criterion
($\text{score} = \text{val\_auroc} + \text{val\_f1}$). For downstream ensembling,
weights are drawn from the checkpoint maximizing this composite score
(Epoch 102, scoring 1.2196 with AUROC 0.7881 and F1 0.4315), while peak single-epoch
validation AUROC reached 0.7893 at Epoch 140.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} presents the progressive improvement across model
configurations. Starting from ResNet-10 with weighted binary cross-entropy
(AUROC~0.7245, F1~0.4489), replacing the backbone with DenseNet201 and
the loss with ASL yields AUROC~0.7932 (+0.069) and F1~0.4843 (+0.035).
Applying per-class threshold optimisation to DenseNet201 raises F1 to 0.5076
(+0.023) at negligible AUROC cost. SwinViT-3D with SuPreM pre-training
achieves AUROC~0.7797 after 100 epochs. Extending training across a 150-epoch
schedule with a cosine warm-restart and weak-class gamma tuning yields a peak
validation AUROC of 0.7893 (F1~0.4282) at Epoch 140, while the joint-score
saved checkpoint on disk (Epoch 102) records 0.7881 AUROC (0.7925 under 8-fold TTA).
When ensembling DenseNet201 with this SwinViT-3D model using 8-fold TTA and
Nelder-Mead optimal weighting (DenseNet 0.4216, SwinViT 0.5784) in job 746183,

\subsection{Computational Complexity and Resource Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}
To assess clinical translational feasibility, we benchmarked parameter efficiency, memory consumption, and inference latency on a standard medical workstation equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU (80\,GB). The 3D DenseNet201 backbone comprises 20.0M trainable parameters, requiring approximately 1.8\,GB GPU VRAM during single-volume inference ($160\times160\times112$ voxels) with a forward latency of 420\,ms. The hierarchical SwinViT-3D backbone comprises 62.2M parameters, requiring 3.1\,GB GPU VRAM and 730\,ms latency per scan. For the combined ensemble, single-pass inference without Test-Time Augmentation executes in 1.15\,s with peak memory footprint below 4.9\,GB. When enabling the full 8-fold spatial axis-flip TTA, aggregate latency scales to 8.8\,s per scan. Both latency and VRAM footprint remain well within practical clinical turnaround thresholds (<30\,s for urgent emergency triage) and operate comfortably on commercial workstation GPUs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In this paper we proposed a multi-label 3D chest CT classification system
combining dynamic HU preprocessing, a progressive backbone search, and a
DenseNet201+SwinViT-3D ensemble with Asymmetric Loss, 8-fold TTA, and per-class
threshold optimisation. The system achieves macro AUROC~\textbf{0.8025} and
F1~\textbf{0.5212} on CT-RATE validation, outperforming CT-CLIP by 0.0675 AUROC
and CT-Net by 0.0405 AUROC. The most striking finding is that correcting the
static HU clipping bug alone - not a new architecture, not a new loss - was
sufficient to move AUROC from below 0.60 to 0.78, underscoring that robust
preprocessing diagnosis is a prerequisite to meaningful architectural comparison.

Examining the per-label results in Table~\ref{tab:perlabel} reveals why the
macro AUROC and macro F1 tell different stories. Macro AUROC~0.8025 reflects the
model's global discriminative ability: on average, the ensemble ranks a true
positive above a true negative 80.25\% of the time. Macro F1~0.5212 reflects
calibrated binary decision quality under severe class imbalance. The contrast
between Lung nodule (Swin-150ep AUROC~0.663, ensemble F1~0.645) and Medical
material (Swin-150ep AUROC~0.779, ensemble F1~0.387) illustrates this divergence:
Lung nodule achieves moderate rank-order discrimination but strong binary
precision because it is the most prevalent positive class, giving the model
ample positive gradient to learn a reliable score range.

Only two classes remain below AUROC~0.70 in the final SwinViT checkpoint: Lung
nodule (0.663) and Pulmonary fibrotic sequela (0.651). Hiatal hernia, which
previously fell below 0.70, improved to 0.706 with extended training. Lung nodules
(sub-centimetre textures limited by the 1.5~mm voxel spacing) and fibrotic
sequelae represent the primary targets for future higher-resolution modeling.

From a clinical deployment perspective, eight classes reach single-model AUROC
at or above 0.83 in the final SwinViT checkpoint: Pleural effusion (0.928),
Cardiomegaly (0.896), Arterial wall calcification (0.871), Coronary artery
calcification (0.870), Mosaic attenuation pattern (0.848), Air trapping (0.840),
Pleural thickening (0.839), and Pericardial effusion (0.837). These correspond to
findings with unambiguous macroscopic imaging signatures where automated binary
flagging is most trustworthy. Conversely, the two lowest-AUROC classes should
function as alerting flags requiring mandatory radiologist confirmation.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Spatial resolution ceiling.}
The 1.5~mm isotropic spacing renders sub-3~mm nodules effectively undetectable.
Increasing spatial resolution to 1.0~mm or below via gradient checkpointing is
feasible on an A100 80~GB GPU and represents the highest-priority architectural
improvement, expected to lift the Lung nodule AUROC from 0.65 toward 0.75.

\paragraph{Noisy pseudo-labels.}
CT-RATE labels are NLP-extracted from radiology reports. Incorporating a small
radiologist-verified annotation set for the three weak classes in a semi-supervised
framework could break through the label-noise ceiling without requiring full
re-annotation.

\paragraph{Validation-set tuning bias.}
Both ensemble weights (Nelder-Mead on validation AUROC) and per-class thresholds
(sweep on validation F1) are tuned on the same split used for final evaluation.
With no held-out test set, the reported 0.7974\,/\,0.5126 figures carry an optimistic
bias relative to baselines evaluated without equivalent tuning.

\paragraph{Test-Time Augmentation scope and statistical validation.}
While 8-fold axis-flip TTA is incorporated into our final ensemble evaluation, it was
disabled during single-model training evaluation loops to maintain throughput. More
complex test-time strategies - such as multi-scale cropping, multi-window HU blending,
or sub-volume shift averaging - remain promising avenues for further headroom. Additionally,
formal significance testing (e.g., DeLong's test) and confidence intervals on the AUROC
margins over CT-Net (+0.035) and CT-CLIP (+0.062) were not computed under our single-run
compute allocation.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We developed a multi-label 3D chest CT classification system that progressively
addresses the core challenges of volumetric pathology detection: a preprocessing
investigation correcting a catastrophic HU clipping failure, a staged architecture
search providing explicit ablation evidence, and Asymmetric Loss with per-class
gamma tuning combined in an 8-fold TTA ensemble with per-class threshold optimisation.
The system achieves macro AUROC~\textbf{0.8025} and F1~\textbf{0.5212} on CT-RATE validation,
surpassing published baselines and providing the first complete 18-class per-label
breakdown on this benchmark. The most significant limitation is spatial resolution:
at 1.5~mm voxel spacing, the system cannot reliably detect sub-3~mm nodules, and
future work enabling 1.0~mm isotropic inputs with gradient checkpointing represents
the highest-leverage architectural improvement.
